The MSc project

A year at Leeds, most of it at a bench. The hardest thing I have done, and the thing I learned the most from.

Getting there

I came to Leeds for an MSc in Infection, Immunology and Human Disease on a Dean’s Scholarship of Excellence, which is awarded on the strength of your undergraduate degree and came with a bursary of £5,000. Mine was for a first class average of 79 per cent in my BSc in biotechnology in India.

Moving from India to the UK for a year of research was the biggest thing I had taken on at that point. A new country, a new system, and a course that expected you to be independent from the first week. The curve was steep, which is exactly what I had come for.

The project

My research project sat in virology. Adenoviruses are used as vectors to carry vaccines into cells, and not long before I started, a rare clotting disorder had been reported in a small number of people who had received an adenovirus-based Covid vaccine. The disorder involves antibodies against platelet factor 4, a small protein released by platelets.

The question I was given was whether the adenovirus capsid itself interacts with platelet factor 4 in a way that could affect how the virus gets into cells.

To get at it, I grew and purified adenovirus type 5, characterised what I had produced, then infected two human cell lines with and without platelet factor 4 present and measured how many cells the virus managed to enter. A year of work sat inside that one sentence.

What I learned to do

The project was built almost entirely out of techniques I had never run before. By the end I was running all of them unsupervised.

Growing and purifying virus

Maintaining 293T cultures, infecting them, and harvesting virus at the right point. Then purification by caesium chloride density gradient ultracentrifugation, which is as finicky as it sounds: the band is either there or it is not, and working out why it is not is most of the skill.

Characterising what came out

SDS-PAGE to see which proteins were present and how clean the preparation really was, and dynamic light scattering to size the particles in solution. Both taught me to be sceptical about my own material before building anything on top of it.

Measuring infection

Transduction assays in two human cell lines using a fluorescent reporter, read on a flow cytometer. Flow is the technique I am most comfortable with now: setting up the run, gating sensibly, and knowing which populations are real and which are debris.

Handling the data

Exporting the results and doing the analysis and plotting in Python, which I picked up during the project because I wanted control over how the figures were made rather than clicking through a wizard. It has been useful in every role since.

The unpredictable bits

Very little of it ran to schedule, which turned out to be the most valuable part.

Cells died and I lost a third of my flasks partway through, which meant rebuilding stocks while the clock kept running. One of the two viruses I was meant to purify never came off the gradient cleanly, so the plan had to change around it. The light scattering was temperamental and I spent more than one Saturday in the building getting readings that were worth having.

Predicting the unpredictable has become something of a mantra for me since, and that year is where it started. Experiments rarely go wrong in the way you prepared for. Learning to keep going, replan quickly and hold the standard anyway is the part that has stayed with me, and it is the part I lean on most in a technician role.

It is also why I am comfortable on the support side of research now. I know what it costs someone when the incubator is out, the stock has run down or a protocol is half written, because I have been the person it cost.
