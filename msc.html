The MSc project

A year at Leeds, most of it at a bench. The hardest thing I have done, and the thing I learned the most from.

Getting there

I came to Leeds for an MSc in Infection, Immunology and Human Disease on a Dean’s Scholarship of Excellence, which is awarded on the strength of your undergraduate degree and came with a bursary of £5,000. Mine was for a first class average of 79 per cent in my BSc in biotechnology in India.

The question

My project sat in virology. Adenoviruses are used as vectors to carry vaccines into cells, and not long before I started, a rare clotting disorder had been reported in a small number of people who had received an adenovirus-based Covid vaccine. The disorder involves antibodies against platelet factor 4, a small protein released by platelets.

The question I was given was whether the adenovirus capsid itself interacts with platelet factor 4 in a way that could matter for how the virus gets into cells.

I worked with adenovirus type 5. I grew it in cell culture, purified it on caesium chloride gradients, checked what I had by dynamic light scattering and SDS-PAGE, then ran transduction assays in two cell lines with and without platelet factor 4 present and read the results by flow cytometry.

The hard parts, and the good ones

A new country and a steep curve

Moving from India to the UK for a year of lab work was the biggest thing I had taken on at that point. A new country, a new system, and a course that expected you to be independent from the first week. The curve was steep and it stayed steep for a while, which is exactly what I had come for.

What I had not expected was how much time I would get with my hands on the equipment. I ran my own cultures, my own gradients, my own gels and my own samples on the flow cytometer, and when something went wrong it was mine to fix. A year of that teaches you more than any amount of reading about it, and it is the reason I can walk into a lab now and be useful on day one.

The bench

Plenty of it did not work. Cells died and I lost a third of my flasks partway through. One of the two viruses I was supposed to purify never came through the gradient cleanly, so I had to work around it. Dynamic light scattering was temperamental and I spent more than one Saturday in the building getting readings that were worth having.

That part I am oddly fond of now. Troubleshooting a preparation that refuses to behave is most of what bench work actually is, and you only learn it by standing there and doing it.

Reading my own results

The hardest part was not the hands, it was the interpretation. I had a set of measurements that mostly showed nothing, a deadline, and not much experience at telling the difference between there is no effect here and this assay could not have detected an effect if there was one. That distinction is the whole thing, and I did not have it yet.

What I would do differently

Looking at it again with a few more years of lab work behind me, three things stand out.

My assay included a control interaction that is well established in the literature, and it did not show up either. At the time I read that as part of the same negative result. It is not. A known positive that fails to appear is telling you the measurement is not sensitive enough, and the right move is to say so plainly and explain why, rather than to treat the silence as an answer.

I ran my transduction experiments in triplicate but presented single values with no spread and no test. Some of the differences I described were well inside the variation between my own repeats. Error bars and a paired test would have changed what I was able to claim, and would have been honest about the rest.

And the conclusion reached further than the data could carry it. What I could support was narrow and specific: under these conditions, with this preparation, no detectable change in transduction. What I wrote was broader than that. Narrowing it would have made the project stronger, not weaker.

I would rather say all of this out loud than leave it sitting in a document nobody reads. Being able to take apart your own work is a more useful skill than having produced flawless work the first time, and I did not have anyone showing me how at the time.

What I took from it

A set of techniques I can still run from memory: cell culture and viral propagation, caesium chloride ultracentrifugation, SDS-PAGE, dynamic light scattering, transduction assays, flow cytometry. A tolerance for things going wrong on a Friday afternoon. And a much better sense of when a result is telling you about biology and when it is telling you about your method.

It is also why I am comfortable in a technician role. I know what it costs someone when the incubator is out, the stock has run down or the protocol is half written, because I have been the person it cost.
